## Fields Not Found — Notes

- **Minimum Commitment:** No standalone minimum revenue or usage commitment in this agreement family. The MRC structure (fixed monthly charges for the committed term) functions as an implicit floor, but no formal commitment clause exists. If Fontara uses all services below their ordered quantities, there is no contractual true-up mechanism.
- **Price Increase (%) / Price Increase Date:** No scheduled escalation. The 2-year rate lock on the SOF is a protective provision, not a price increase schedule. Any increase requires 30 days' notice and only takes effect at renewal or as a supplier pass-through. No % cap defined for renewal-period increases.
- **Service Activation Date:** This field cannot be extracted at signing — it is a post-provisioning operational event. The documents define target installation intervals (30 days from Order Date for most services) and the Billing Start Date trigger, but the actual activation date is confirmed only when Provider completes provisioning.

## Conflicts & Ambiguities

---

### 1. Term — Direct Conflict Across Documents

| | |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | SOF selects a **2-year Committed Term** (§6.1); MSA (§8.1.1), Fee Schedule (§6.1.1), and SLA (§10.1.1) each set **1-year initial terms** |
| **Clause in tension** | MSA hierarchy clause (§1) states MSA governs conflicts — but the SOF's term selection is a commercial election that should logically control for services |
| **Downstream risk** | Billing, Renewal, and Early Termination RA teams may load different term lengths depending on which document they reference |

**Why this happens in the real world:** A renewal team sees the MSA expiring after year 1 and sends a non-renewal notice. Billing keeps invoicing because the SOF says 2-year committed term. Or the reverse — the customer tries to exit in month 13 assuming the MSA renewed, and the provider invokes the SOF committed term to collect ETF. Disputes over which document's term controls are among the most common commercial contract litigation scenarios in managed services.

---

### 2. SOF Rate Lock vs. MSA Fee Adjustment Right

| | |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | SOF §8.1 locks all MRC rates for **24 months**; MSA §4.2 permits Provider to modify fees at any Renewal Term with **30 days' written notice** |
| **Clause in tension** | MSA's own initial term is 1 year — it renews before the SOF's 2-year lock expires, giving Provider a plausible argument that MSA renewal rights allow fee increases in year 2 |
| **Downstream risk** | The rate lock Subscriber negotiated in the SOF may not be enforceable in year 2; the MSA hierarchy clause would favor Provider's reading |

**Why this happens in the real world:** Provider sends a rate increase notice in month 13 citing MSA §4.2 renewal rights. Customer cites the SOF rate lock. Provider says MSA governs in conflicts. This plays out in telecom disputes routinely — and providers often prevail because the MSA hierarchy clause is drafted by their legal team to favor exactly this outcome. Pricing & Revenue Assurance teams get caught off guard when a "locked" account suddenly has a rate change they weren't expecting.

---

### 3. Termination for Convenience vs. ETF — Ambiguity Within the MSA

| | |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | MSA §8.3 allows either party to terminate **for any reason** on 30 days' notice; MSA §4.3 imposes ETF for early termination for any reason **other than Provider's uncured material breach** — convenience terminations are not carved out |
| **Clause in tension** | Both clauses live in the same document without reconciliation |
| **Downstream risk** | A Subscriber invoking §8.3 could argue ETF doesn't apply; Provider could invoke §4.3; Early Termination RA and Billing may invoice ETF without knowing the customer has a viable counter-argument |

**Why this happens in the real world:** Customer invokes the convenience exit clause, declines to pay ETF, provider sends the balance to collections. The ambiguity is often intentional on the provider's side — §8.3 is written to look broad during the sales process while §4.3 quietly preserves ETF collection. Billing and finance teams typically only see the ETF schedule and assume it's owed, without visibility to the §8.3 argument on the other side.

---

### 4. SLA Execution Lag — Services Ordered Before SLA Was Signed

| | |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | SOF executed May 22 (triggers provisioning); SLA not executed until June 21 — **30 days later** |
| **Execution sequence** | MSA: May 10 → SOF: May 22 → Fee Schedule: June 11 → SLA: June 21 |
| **Downstream risk** | Any outage or installation failure during the May 22–June 21 window may be disputed; Provider could deny SLA credits citing no executed SLA at the time |

**Why this happens in the real world:** An outage occurs during the gap window and the customer files an SLA credit claim. Provider denies it citing no executed SLA at the time of the incident. Also relevant for Fulfillment: if the 30-day installation interval starts from Order Date (May 22) but the SLA wasn't signed until June 21, Provider could argue the installation clock isn't contractually bound until the SLA is in effect. Fulfillment and SLA Performance teams should treat **June 21** as the earliest defensible SLA start date for this agreement family.
